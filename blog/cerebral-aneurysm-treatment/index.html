Cerebral Aneurysm: Clipping, Coiling and When Surgery Is Needed

21 August 2026 · 8 min read · Prof. Guive Sharifi

Cerebral aneurysms require careful diagnosis and treatment. This article explains clipping, coiling, and when surgery is recommended to manage this condition effectively.

Share this post

A cerebral aneurysm is a weak or thin spot on a blood vessel in the brain that balloons out and fills with blood. This condition can be life-threatening if the aneurysm ruptures, causing bleeding in the brain known as subarachnoid haemorrhage. Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial to prevent rupture and serious complications. Treatment options vary depending on the aneurysm’s size, location, and the patient’s overall health. Two common methods are aneurysm clipping and endovascular coiling, both aiming to prevent bleeding by securing the aneurysm.

Understanding cerebral aneurysm treatment is essential for patients and families facing this diagnosis. Your neurosurgeon will carefully assess your condition to recommend the safest and most effective approach. This article explains the key treatment options, when surgery may be necessary, and what patients can expect during recovery. With advances in neurosurgery, many patients achieve good outcomes with minimally invasive techniques and expert care.

What Is a Cerebral Aneurysm?

A cerebral aneurysm occurs when a blood vessel in the brain develops a bulge due to a weakened wall. This bulge can grow over time and may eventually rupture, leading to bleeding in the brain. The most common site for aneurysms is at the branching points of arteries in the circle of Willis, a critical area supplying blood to the brain. Many aneurysms are small and asymptomatic, discovered incidentally during imaging for other reasons.

However, larger aneurysms or those that leak blood can cause symptoms such as severe headache, vision changes, or neurological deficits. A ruptured aneurysm causes subarachnoid haemorrhage, a medical emergency with high risk of stroke, brain damage, or death. Prompt treatment is vital to secure the aneurysm and prevent further bleeding.

Aneurysm Clipping: Surgical Treatment Explained

Aneurysm clipping is a traditional surgical method to treat cerebral aneurysms. During this procedure, a neurosurgeon performs a craniotomy, opening the skull to access the brain. The surgeon places a tiny metal clip at the base of the aneurysm to stop blood flow into the bulge. This prevents the aneurysm from rupturing or bleeding again. Clipping is often recommended for aneurysms that are accessible and have a suitable shape for this approach.

While clipping requires open brain surgery, it offers a permanent solution by physically isolating the aneurysm from circulation. Recovery may involve a hospital stay of several days and gradual return to normal activities. Your surgeon will discuss the risks and benefits based on your specific aneurysm characteristics and overall health.

Endovascular Coiling: A Minimally Invasive Option

Endovascular coiling is a less invasive treatment for cerebral aneurysms. Instead of opening the skull, the neurosurgeon inserts a thin catheter through an artery in the groin and guides it to the aneurysm in the brain. Tiny platinum coils are then released into the aneurysm, filling the space and promoting blood clotting inside the bulge. This effectively seals off the aneurysm from blood flow and reduces the risk of rupture.

Coiling is often preferred for aneurysms that are difficult to reach with open surgery or for patients who may not tolerate a craniotomy. The procedure usually requires less recovery time and hospital stay. However, some aneurysms may need follow-up imaging and additional treatment if coils do not completely seal the aneurysm.

When Is Surgery Needed for Cerebral Aneurysms?

Not all cerebral aneurysms require immediate surgery. Many small, unruptured aneurysms can be monitored with regular imaging and managed conservatively if the risk of rupture is low. Surgery or endovascular treatment is typically recommended when the aneurysm is large, growing, symptomatic, or has ruptured causing subarachnoid haemorrhage.

Your neurosurgeon will evaluate factors such as aneurysm size, location, patient age, and overall health to decide the best treatment plan. In cases of rupture, emergency intervention is critical to prevent life-threatening bleeding and complications. Elective surgery may be planned for unruptured aneurysms with higher risk features to prevent future rupture.

Risks and Recovery After Cerebral Aneurysm Treatment

Both aneurysm clipping and endovascular coiling carry risks, including bleeding, infection, stroke, or neurological deficits. Your surgical team will take every precaution to minimize these risks and monitor you closely during and after the procedure. Recovery times vary depending on the treatment type and individual patient factors.

After clipping surgery, patients may spend several days in the hospital and require rehabilitation to regain strength and neurological function. Endovascular coiling usually allows faster recovery with shorter hospital stays. Follow-up imaging is essential to ensure the aneurysm remains sealed and to detect any recurrence early. Your neurosurgeon will guide you through the recovery process and recommend lifestyle changes to support brain health.

  • Aneurysm clipping involves open brain surgery with a metal clip placement.
  • Endovascular coiling is minimally invasive using catheter-delivered coils.
  • Surgery is needed for large, symptomatic, or ruptured aneurysms.
  • Small, stable aneurysms may be monitored without immediate surgery.
  • Risks include bleeding, infection, and stroke, managed by expert care.
  • Recovery varies: longer after clipping, shorter after coiling.
  • Follow-up imaging is critical to monitor treatment success.
  • Lifestyle changes can reduce future aneurysm risks.

Frequently Asked Questions About Cerebral Aneurysm Treatment

What symptoms might indicate a cerebral aneurysm?

Many cerebral aneurysms do not cause symptoms until they grow large or rupture. Warning signs can include sudden severe headache often described as the worst headache ever, vision problems, neck pain, nausea, or neurological changes such as weakness or numbness. If you experience these symptoms, seek immediate medical attention as they may indicate aneurysm rupture or leakage.

How do doctors decide between clipping and coiling?

The choice between clipping and coiling depends on aneurysm size, shape, location, and patient health. Clipping is preferred for aneurysms accessible by open surgery and with a neck shape suitable for clipping. Coiling is often chosen for aneurysms in difficult-to-reach areas or for patients who may not tolerate open surgery. Your neurosurgeon will assess imaging studies and discuss the best option for your case.

Is cerebral aneurysm treatment risky?

All treatments carry some risk, including bleeding, infection, stroke, or neurological complications. However, these risks are balanced against the danger of aneurysm rupture, which can be fatal. Experienced neurosurgeons use advanced techniques to minimize risks and improve outcomes. Your surgeon will explain potential risks and benefits before treatment.

What is the recovery like after aneurysm surgery?

Recovery varies by treatment type and patient condition. After clipping surgery, hospital stay may last several days with gradual return to normal activities over weeks to months. Endovascular coiling usually allows quicker recovery with shorter hospital stays. Rehabilitation may be needed to regain strength or neurological function. Follow-up care includes imaging to monitor the aneurysm site.

Can cerebral aneurysms be prevented?

While not all aneurysms can be prevented, certain lifestyle changes may reduce risk. These include controlling high blood pressure, avoiding smoking, managing cholesterol, and maintaining a healthy diet and exercise routine. Regular medical check-ups can help detect aneurysms early, especially if you have a family history or risk factors. Your neurosurgeon can provide personalized advice for prevention.
